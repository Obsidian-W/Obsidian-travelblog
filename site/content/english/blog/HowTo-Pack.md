For most trips, one week of clothing and a regular laundry stop are enough
A lighter backpack is easier to carry between stations, hostels and hiking trails. This is the compact packing list I use.

> OK, so it's a basic article, but I bet a useful one, here are my packing advices
> At the bottom I'll tell you what exactly I pak every time + seasonal stuff

---

## Clothes

- One week worth of clothes, max. I do a load of laundry every week (So technically, 8 days of clothes? I like to have ambiguous clothing for my last day like pajama/sport shirt).  
- One warmer layer (hoodie, fleece, whatever) for nights, cold climates or higher altitudes.  
- Underwear and socks can get dirty fast, bring enough to rotate. (Socks are fine if you don't sweat with your feet, I don't)
- Shoes: one solid pair for walking, eventually flip flops or something similar. (Sandals can be nice as well, unless you're hiking)
- Sunglasses / Cap

> Note: You'll notice that everybody wear the same clothes a couple time, if you don't sweat too much, use a t-shirt for two days.
> If you can't wash a cloth right away, air dry it so it remains 'fresh', then pack it aside your other clothes.
> Drink enough water, you don't want to have sweat touching your clothes, otherwise they will smell.
> Showers twice a day if possible, it also help maintaining clean clothes.

Note: Choose clothes that can be washed together without separating colours, and clothes with proper material that don't keep smell.
Always wear clothes adapted to the weather, in hot weather linen is a godsent.

## Sleep & Comfort

- Sleeping mask: useful on planes, buses and in shared rooms. Especially when a-holes turn on the light at 4AM.
- Earplugs: useful in shared rooms unless, like me, you are hearing impaired (And even then, I still do use them, sometimes).
- A neck pillow: Optional, personally I don't like them, but if you sleep seated, it's quite good. But takes too much space.

---

## Health

- Bandaids and sterile compresses: I seldom use them for myself, but I saved a few with them.
- Sun screen. **VERY Important**, cancer is a bitch.
- Condoms.
- Diarrhea medication: carry the medication you normally use rather than relying on finding the same product abroad. I personally take **Imodium**.
- Doliprane or similar.

> For the last points, consult your physician, that's better. Don't take medication unless really needed too, if you got the diarrhea, it's for a reason.
> But please, consult whenever needed.

If you got space, a thermometer can be useful too, to know if you got a real fever and when you really need to take a break/see a doctor.

Health is the **most important thing**, take care.

Hygiene goes second, even if you travel, stay clean, it helps to keep clothes/bedding clean and your health too.

##  Hygiene

- Wet wipes: Replace Toilet Paper or showers when camping without water.
- Antibacterial gel: use it often, especially before eating. If going to dirty places, you might even have to resupply along the way. (I buy 50ml, very small so it's convenient to carry)
- Dry soap and shampoo: small, doesn’t leak, often ecological.
- Deodorant that is not anti-perspirant. (I use an oat deodorant from Yves Rocher, it allows me to sweat with a good smell (But drinky plenty to))
- Toothbrush and toothpaste. (Obviously! For toothpaste, I use small 100ml package, they last for bigger trips)
- File/Nail trimmer. (After two weeks of traveling without using your nail, you'll feel the need)
- Lightweight towel: Dries fast and doesn’t take too much space. Useful when ho(s)tels don't provide any.

---

## Electronics

- USB cables, at least two, just in case. I take two 3.1 type C (one longer, one shorter for emergencies), And a 2.0 Type A (For my power bank)
- Power bank: useful on long journeys and where charging points are limited. I recommend taking one that is more than 10k mAh
- Adapter: You can find universal blocks online, some even charge multiple devices at once.
- Reader: A Kindle or a Kobo are nice things to have, lighter than books and they last a while, battery-wise.
- Earphones: useful in shared rooms and on public transport. Sometimes I even take my headset (A Plantronics BB Pro 2).

- Foldable hiking poles: Ok, that take some space, but as I'm getting older, I'm becoming more fragile while going down slopes. It's also useful when hiking above 5000 meters.

## ID

- Passport.
- ID Card if  you don't use the passport. (Taking both means having a chance of losing both)
- Driving license/International driving licence (If you plan to drive)

## The Rule

If an item has no clear use on the planned route, leave it behind. Most ordinary toiletries and clothing can be replaced during the trip, although specialised equipment may be harder to find.

Travel light and keep it simple: every unnecessary kilo feels ten times heavier after a week.
