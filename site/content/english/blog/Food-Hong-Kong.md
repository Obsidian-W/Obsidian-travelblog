## Dim sum and Cantonese basics

![Dim sum in Hong Kong](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Dim%20Sum%20Breakfast.jpg)

Dim sum is the easiest first Hong Kong meal if you are not travelling alone. Order several small dishes, tea and a few steamed or fried standards. The Hong Kong Tourism Board lists beginner dishes such as **siu mai**, **cheung fun** and **egg tarts**.

| Food | What it is | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Siu mai | Pork and shrimp dumplings | Dim sum restaurants |
| Har gow | Shrimp dumplings | Dim sum restaurants |
| Cheung fun | Rice noodle rolls | Dim sum and local restaurants |
| Char siu bao | Barbecue pork buns | Dim sum restaurants and bakeries |
| Egg tart | Custard tart | Bakeries and dim sum restaurants |

Go earlier for dim sum. Late lunch can mean fewer dishes or longer waits.

## Wonton noodles and roast meats

**Wonton noodles** are a practical solo meal: fast, clear and easy to find. Look for shops with a short noodle menu rather than restaurants doing every Cantonese dish. Roast goose, roast duck, char siu and crispy pork are another simple order; choose busy shops where meat is turning over quickly.

Yuen Long has older noodle institutions, but you do not need to cross the city only for one bowl. Good noodle shops exist across Kowloon and Hong Kong Island.

## Cha chaan teng

**Cha chaan teng** are Hong Kong-style cafes. They are useful for breakfast, late meals and cheap breaks: milk tea, pineapple buns, macaroni soup, eggs, toast, pork chop rice, instant noodles and baked rice.

The food is not delicate. The point is speed, price and local rhythm. Expect shared tables, quick service and menus that mix Cantonese and Western-influenced dishes.

## Dai pai dong and cooked-food markets

![Dai pai dong in Hong Kong](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Sing%20Kee%20Dai%20Pai%20Dong%20in%20Central%20Hong%20Kong.jpg)

For open-air or cooked-food-market meals, target places with strong turnover. The Hong Kong Tourism Board lists dai pai dong and cooked-food market options in Central, Tsim Sha Tsui, Jordan, Tai Hang and Sham Shui Po, with dishes such as tomato noodles, beef balls, salt-and-pepper squid, milk tea and pork chop noodles.

These places can be crowded and direct. Do not arrive expecting quiet service. Order a few dishes, share, and keep valuables off the table edge.

## Street snacks and markets

Mong Kok, Causeway Bay, Sham Shui Po and market streets are good for small food: curry fish balls, egg waffles, beef offal, siu mai on sticks, herbal tea, bakery buns and fruit drinks. This is where it makes sense to graze rather than sit down.

Street snacks are best hot and busy. If a stall looks quiet for too long, choose another.

## Dietary notes

Vegetarians can eat in Hong Kong, but traditional Cantonese kitchens often use oyster sauce, shrimp paste, lard or meat stock. Buddhist vegetarian restaurants are easier if you want confidence.

Gluten-free travel is difficult because soy sauce, noodles, dumpling wrappers and bakery items are common. Rice, plain steamed dishes and some dedicated restaurants help, but ask carefully.
