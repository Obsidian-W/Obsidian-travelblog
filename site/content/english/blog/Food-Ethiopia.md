## Injera and how meals work

![Injera in Ethiopia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Eating%20Injera%2C%20Ethiopia%20%2814683490675%29.jpg)

Most Ethiopian meals are built around **injera**, the sour flatbread that works as plate, starch and utensil. Stews, vegetables and meat are served on top or beside it. Tear off a piece of injera with your right hand and use it to pick up food.

Injera is traditionally made with teff, but restaurants may mix teff with wheat, barley or other grains. Gluten-free travellers should ask before assuming it is pure teff. The flavour is sour; if that is difficult at first, order rice or bread when available and use injera for the stews.

## Core stews and fasting plates

The most useful first order is **beyaynetu**, a mixed fasting plate. It usually brings several meat-free dishes on one injera: lentils, split peas, greens, potatoes, cabbage, beetroot or shiro. It is common in local restaurants and especially useful on Orthodox fasting days.

| Food | What it is | Where it is strongest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shiro | Chickpea or broad-bean stew, often smooth and spiced | Everywhere; simple restaurants and fasting menus |
| Misir wat | Red lentil stew with berbere | Local restaurants, mixed vegetarian platters |
| Kik alicha | Mild yellow split-pea stew | Fasting plates and milder meals |
| Gomen | Cooked greens, sometimes with onion and garlic | Vegetarian platters and side dishes |
| Atkilt wat | Cabbage, potato and carrot stew | Fasting plates and everyday restaurants |

These dishes are the safest way to eat well without chasing named restaurants. Busy lunch places with high turnover are usually better than empty traditional restaurants aimed only at visitors.

## Meat dishes

Ethiopian meat dishes are good, but order them where turnover is clear. **Doro wat** is the classic chicken stew with berbere, usually served with egg and injera. It is common in traditional restaurants, but it is more of a prepared dish than a fast lunch.

| Food | What it is | Practical note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doro wat | Chicken stew with berbere and egg | Best in traditional restaurants or family-style places |
| Tibs | Fried or sauteed meat, often beef or lamb | Common for lunch or dinner; good with injera |
| Kitfo | Minced beef with spice and clarified butter | Often served raw or lightly cooked; ask for cooked if needed |
| Key wat | Spicy red meat stew | Stronger berbere flavour |
| Alicha wat | Milder meat stew | Easier if you want less chilli |

Raw or lightly cooked meat exists in Ethiopia, including kitfo and tere siga. It is part of the food culture, but it is not a low-risk choice for travellers. If you want kitfo, order it **leb leb** or fully cooked.

## Breakfast and snacks

Breakfast is more varied than many short guides suggest. **Firfir** is torn injera mixed with sauce, often spicy and filling. **Chechebsa** or **kita firfir** is torn flatbread mixed with spiced butter or sauce. **Genfo** is a thick porridge, usually eaten with spiced butter in the centre. **Ful** is common in cities and is useful when you want a cheaper, less injera-heavy breakfast.

For snacks, look for **sambusa**, fresh bread, simple cakes, fruit juice and tea. **Spris** is a layered fruit juice, often avocado, mango and papaya. It is filling enough to count as a small meal.

## Addis Ababa

Addis is the easiest place to eat broadly: local restaurants, fasting plates, meat restaurants, coffee houses, hotel dining rooms and modern cafes are all available. It is the best place to learn the menu before smaller towns.

For a first meal, order beyaynetu or shiro. For meat, choose tibs or cooked kitfo in a busy place. For coffee, Addis has both traditional coffee ceremonies and normal cafes, so you do not need to force a formal ceremony on a travel day.

Current online menus are useful only as a reference, not as a national price list. In Addis Ababa menus checked in August 2026, simple fasting dishes and shiro were commonly listed around **650-900 ETB**, firfir and mixed vegetarian combinations around **1,000-1,800 ETB**, and meat dishes such as tibs, kitfo and doro wat around **1,100-2,250 ETB**. Delivery platforms and well-known Bole restaurants can be higher than small local restaurants, so use these as city benchmarks, not fixed travel costs.

## Northern highlands

In Lalibela, Gondar and Bahir Dar, expect injera meals, shiro, tibs, firfir, simple hotel restaurants and fasting plates around religious areas. Food choice may shrink when visitor numbers are low, during instability or during fuel and supply disruptions.

The northern highlands are also where **tej**, Ethiopian honey wine, is easiest to encounter in restaurants or tej houses. Try it carefully; strength and sweetness vary, and quality is uneven.

## Harar and the east

Harar has a different food context from the northern highlands: Muslim heritage, market snacks, tea, coffee, bread and meat dishes. Look for fresh turnover rather than specific restaurant names. The old city is compact, and practical eating often means simple local restaurants near the market or your guesthouse.

Dire Dawa and the eastern lowlands add more Somali-influenced food: rice, pasta, meat, spiced tea and flatter breads are easier to find than in the northern highlands. This is useful if you need a break from injera.

## Coffee and drinks

![Ethiopian coffee ceremony](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Ethiopian%20coffee%20ceremony%20-%20Addis%20Ababa.jpg)

Coffee is one of the strongest reasons to pay attention to food in Ethiopia. A traditional ceremony takes time: roasting, grinding, brewing in a jebena and serving small cups. Do it when you have a slow afternoon, not before a bus or flight.

Tea is common. Fresh juices are easy in Addis and larger towns. Bottled water is the default for travellers.

## Dietary notes

Vegetarians usually do well because Orthodox fasting traditions create many meat-free dishes. Ask for **yetsom** food or fasting food. Vegan travellers should still ask about niter kibbeh, the spiced clarified butter used in some dishes.

If you need mild food, ask for **alicha** dishes, shiro, rice, bread, lentils or vegetables. Berbere-heavy stews can be hot, but Ethiopian food is not uniformly spicy.
