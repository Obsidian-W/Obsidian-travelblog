## Injera and how meals work

![Injera in Ethiopia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Eating%20Injera%2C%20Ethiopia%20%2814683490675%29.jpg)

Most Ethiopian meals are built around **injera**, the sour flatbread that works as plate, starch and utensil. Stews, vegetables and meat are served on top or beside it. Tear off a piece of injera with your main hand and use it to pick up food.

Injera is traditionally made with teff (an uncommon cereal) but restaurants may mix teff with wheat, barley or other grains, apparently (Found that info online).
Thus, gluten-free travellers should ask before assuming it is pure teff. The flavour is sour.
Honestly, I found Injera pretty disgusting, I can't stand sour tastes. So I suffered for my three weeks and a half I had in Ethiopia.

## Core stews and fasting plates

The most useful first order is **beyaynetu**, a mixed fasting plate. It usually brings several meat-free dishes on one injera: lentils, split peas, greens, potatoes, cabbage, beetroot or shiro. It is common in local restaurants and especially useful on Orthodox fasting days.

| Food | What it is | Noe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shiro | Chickpea or broad-bean stew, often smooth and spiced | Pretty simple and tasty, served along Injera|
| Misir wat | Red lentil stew with berbere | Nicely spiced, filled with fiber and proteins  |

## Meat dishes

Ethiopian meat dishes are good, but order them where turnover is clear. **Doro wat** is the classic chicken stew with berbere, usually served with egg and injera. It is common in traditional restaurants, but it is more of a prepared dish than a fast lunch.

| Food | What it is | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doro wat | Chicken stew with berbere and egg | Found everywhere, makes a good lunch|
| Tibs | Fried or sauteed meat, often beef or lamb | Very common for lunch or dinner |
| Kitfo | Minced beef with spice and clarified butter | Often served raw or lightly cooked; ask for cooked if needed |
| Key wat | Spicy red meat stew | Couldn't find it |
| Alicha wat | Milder meat stew | Couldn't find it |

Raw or lightly cooked meat exists in Ethiopia, including kitfo. It is part of the food culture, but it is not a low-risk choice for travellers. If you want kitfo lightly cooked, ask for **leb leb**; you can also request it fully cooked. If you want kitfo, prepare your anus.

## Breakfast and snacks

Breakfast is more varied than many short guides suggest. **Firfir** is torn injera mixed with sauce, often spicy and filling. **Chechebsa** or **kita firfir** is torn flatbread mixed with spiced butter or sauce. **Genfo** is a thick porridge, usually eaten with spiced butter in the centre. **Ful** is common in cities and is useful when you want a cheaper, less injera-heavy breakfast.

IMHO, breakfasts are the best part of Ethiopia's food culture, mostly because you do without the Injera.

For snacks, look for **sambusa**, fresh bread, simple cakes, fruit juice and tea. **Spris** is a layered fruit juice, often avocado, mango and papaya. It is filling enough to count as a small meal.

## Addis Ababa

Addis is the easiest place to eat broadly: local restaurants, fasting plates, meat restaurants, coffee houses, hotel dining rooms and modern cafes are all available. 

For a first meal, order Injera. It's important to know right away if you can make it.
Because Injera twice a day is boring.

For coffee, Addis has both traditional coffee ceremonies and normal cafes, so you do not need to force a formal ceremony on a travel day.

For breakfasts, they are often served in coffee places.

Current online menus are useful only as a reference, not as a national price list. In Addis Ababa menus checked in August 2026, simple fasting dishes and shiro were commonly listed around **650-900 ETB**, firfir and mixed vegetarian combinations around **1,000-1,800 ETB**, and meat dishes such as tibs, kitfo and doro wat around **1,100-2,250 ETB**. Delivery platforms and well-known Bole restaurants can be higher than small local restaurants.

## Northern highlands

In Lalibela, Gondar and Bahir Dar, expect injera meals, shiro, tibs, firfirand fasting plates around religious areas. Food choice may shrink when visitor numbers are low, during instability or during fuel and supply disruptions (ie: Always, lol)

## Coffee and drinks

![Ethiopian coffee ceremony](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Ethiopian%20coffee%20ceremony%20-%20Addis%20Ababa.jpg)

A traditional ceremony takes time: roasting, grinding, brewing in a jebena and serving small cups. You can get coffee pretty much everywhere, even the side of the road

Tea is common. Fresh juices are easy in Addis and larger towns. Bottled water is the default for travellers.

> You can't drink tap water in Ethiopia

## Dietary notes

Vegetarians usually do well because Orthodox fasting traditions create many meat-free dishes. Ask for **yetsom** food or fasting food. Vegan travellers should still ask about niter kibbeh, the spiced clarified butter used in some dishes.

If you need mild food, ask for **alicha** dishes, shiro, rice, bread, lentils or vegetables. Berbere-heavy stews can be hot, but Ethiopian food is not uniformly spicy.

> Good luck with the food, if you like Injera, you'll be rewarded with one of the world's top food.
