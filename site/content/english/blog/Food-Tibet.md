## Barley, dairy and meat

![Tibetan butter tea](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Tibetan%20butter%20tea.jpg)

Highland barley survives where rice and many vegetables do not. Herds provide meat, butter and cheese. The result is a cuisine built around **tsampa**, tea, dairy, mutton, yak or beef, dumplings and soup—not the huge chilli-and-stir-fry menu many travellers associate with China.

Lhasa gives the widest choice. Once you leave for Yamdrok, Gyantse, Shigatse or Everest, meals become simpler: noodle shops, rice dishes, dumplings, eggs, packaged snacks and whatever your guide knows is open that day.

## Lhasa tea houses

**Po cha**, or butter tea, is tea churned with salt and butter until the fat emulsifies. It is hot, savoury and richer than ordinary milk tea. Try a small cup first, as the salty flavour is unfamiliar to many visitors.

Lhasa **sweet tea** combines black tea, milk and sugar and resembles South Asian milk tea. Tea houses sell it by the cup or flask and are everyday social spaces rather than formal tea rooms.

Around Barkhor, tea houses function much like communal living rooms. If you want to visit one, mention it to the guide before the day's route is fixed; group tours otherwise tend to stop at larger restaurants that can seat everyone together.

## Tsampa

**Tsampa** is flour made from barley that has already been roasted. Because it is precooked, it needs no baking: add a small amount of butter tea, then squeeze and knead the mixture in the bowl until it holds together. Add the tea gradually to control the texture.

The flavour is toasted and nutty. Its portability and high energy content explain why it became such an important staple.

## Noodles, dumplings and simple meals

**Thukpa** is the broad family of noodle soups. **Thenthuk** uses pieces of dough pulled and torn directly into the broth, giving it thick, irregular noodles with more chew. **Momos** are steamed or fried dumplings, usually filled with meat or vegetables. **Tingmo** is coiled steamed bread with no filling; tear it apart and use it to absorb stew.

Noodle soup is a practical individual order, while hotpot is generally intended for sharing. On road journeys, eat at the planned meal stops because kitchens may be closed by the time the group reaches its accommodation.

## Yak, mutton and dairy

Yak, beef or mutton appears in hotpot, stews and dumplings, while yak is also sold in dried strips. Dried meat can be very firm and chewy. Tibetan yoghurt is thick and sharply sour and is often served with sugar; dried cheese and other dairy products are also common.

“Yak” on an English tourist menu can be difficult to verify. Treat it as a local meat dish rather than paying a large premium solely for the label.

## Shigatse and road towns

Shigatse has a much wider choice of restaurants than the smaller settlements on the Everest road. Sichuan restaurants provide rice, vegetables and stir-fries alongside Tibetan dishes.

## On the road to Yamdrok, Shigatse and Everest

Road meals are commonly limited to noodles, fried rice, dumplings, eggs and tea. Use the scheduled lunch stop, as the next settlement may not have an open kitchen.

Bring snacks from Lhasa before leaving for Yamdrok, Shigatse or Everest. Nuts, biscuits, fruit, instant oatmeal and rehydration salts travel well. Fresh fruits and vegetarian dishes are not available at every stop.

For Everest-side itineraries, ask your agency what meals are included before departure. Some guesthouses and tented stops have only basic kitchens. You'll probably just have some noodles, anyway. In cold weather, reliable hot water and soup matter more than menu variety.

## Vegetarian and dietary notes

Vegetarian food is quite possible in Lhasa, especially around tourist restaurants and some monastery areas, but road routes are harder. Ask clearly about broth, meat oil and dumpling filling. Vegan travel is more difficult because dairy is common.

> Thanks to Buddhism, vegetarian will have it relatively easy.

If you avoid gluten, Tibet is not easy: noodles, dumplings, bread and barley are common. Rice and simple vegetable dishes can help, but availability drops outside Lhasa.

## Ordering notes

Foreign visitors travel on an approved route with a guide, so food stops are partly chosen in advance. Tell the agency before departure if you want local tea houses, small restaurants or particular dishes rather than standard group restaurants.

Carry cash for small stops. Mobile payment should work (Alipay and Wechat are pretty much everywhere), but I found out that cash was still used in Tibet.
