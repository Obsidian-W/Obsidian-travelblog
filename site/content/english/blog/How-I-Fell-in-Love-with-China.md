## One country, many cultures, many faces

China is not one uniform culture. From Yunnan to Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, and the coastal megacities, languages, food, religions, architecture, and daily habits can change dramatically. Since the reform-and-opening period associated with Deng Xiaoping, economic development has transformed the country, while many traditional practices have remained visible or found new audiences. Temples are not only museums: in many places, young people still visit them, burn incense, and take part in festivals.

The country officially recognises 55 ethnic minority groups, and minority communities historically had different family-planning rules from the Han majority. Tibetan and Chinese can both be seen on public signs in Tibet, and minority languages have legal protection. Still, this needs nuance: state support and cultural revival do not erase restrictions or political tensions. The situations in Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia, especially regarding surveillance, language policy, travel, and political expression, deserve careful treatment rather than a simple “protected minorities” narrative.

## A protected nature and culture

China also surprised me with its efforts to protect and present nature. From major city parks—including Shenzhen’s large green spaces—to national parks and mountain reserves, greenery is not treated as an afterthought. At Jade Dragon Snow Mountain, signs constantly remind visitors not to leave trash, light fires, or smoke. The message is clear: these places are meant to stay beautiful.

The same effort is visible in cities. Parks, flowers, planted avenues, and landscaped public spaces are everywhere, even in dense urban areas. It does not solve every environmental problem, but it makes the country feel much greener than many people expect.

## Fantastic food and where to find it

One of the things I love most about China is that fantastic food is everywhere: diverse and regularly awesome, from street food to fast food, roadside restaurants, and finally nicer ones. You have everything.

The first time I went to China, in Yunnan, I was slightly disappointed at first.
Food at roadside restaurants was oily, snacks in Seven-Elevens were heavily processed and full of additives...
Then, I went out of the cheap stuff. Because, yes, those "roadside restaurants" were cheap eats at less than 2€ a dish. Snacks at Seven-Elevens were cheap and mostly useful to bring on the road.
I went out to the food market in Kunming, large and filled with people, the price was higher, the choice broader and the quality higher.

Just a sample of what you find in Kunming food markets:

- Chicken skewers on a whole lemongrass stick, rat skewers, Seitan skewers with cumin...
- Takoyaki, sausageyaki, rice flour balls (IDk how they are called, you can find them in Madagascar too as Mofo Gasy, they use the same takoyaki machinery)...
- Rice cakes, corn on the cob, sweet potatoes...
- Mochi and rose flower cakes...

This is just like, 20% of what you can find in a food market, more complete dishes are also available.
A lot of variety, often for good prices (You can make a meal out of 4 to 5€).

Otherwise, I discovered fast food. Oily as well, but a nice thing to try in China; it also costs 4 to 5€ and is common in malls or near city squares. Often more Western or more Japanese, it offers another kind of variety.

Then you have nicer restaurants, with proper service, they can often cost up to 10€ for a meal, but you'll find more refined dishes that you can't find everywhere.

And finally: hole-in-the-walls.
Better than what I call roadside restaurants (which are an in-between, often found near commercial roads or where workers go).
Those are tiny places, often on the ground floor of the house their owners live in.
Sometimes, they don't even have a menu, or when they have one, it's very small.
They are some of the best restaurants, a bit hidden, a bit farther out than the others, sometimes you walk randomly and stumble upon them. Real treasures.

### The Great Firewall

Currently using the internet with a Chinese SIM card / network sucks...
Imagine having no access to tools and apps from Meta, Google, Microsoft...
It's that bad. Working with teams in China means that they need to use VPNs all the time, which slows things down and adds a layer of complexity to every exchange.
Travelling in China means that Google Maps is barely efficient; Booking.com and other booking apps are limited (Thankfully, hostels targeting foreigners do use Booking.com and some other tools).
Additionally, not all VPNs work in China; you need to choose wisely.

Personally, I just go to China with a French SIM card, since I get 30 GB/month of internet without a hint of censorship.

But censorship sucks. It's not protecting anyone; it's slowing down international cooperation and is an additional hardship for tourists.
